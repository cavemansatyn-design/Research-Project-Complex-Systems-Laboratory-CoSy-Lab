## 1. Background and Motivation

Following the presentation of our initial 39-page CocktailDB research report, we moved into a refinement phase.

The original implementation had already reproduced the major analytical components of the paper, including Zipf's law, Heaps' law, the Menzerath–Altmann relationship and nutrient-distribution analysis.

It had also introduced ingredient co-occurrence networks, PMI analysis, correlation matrices and PCA.

However, the first implementation raised several questions about the robustness and interpretation of the results.

A particularly important realization was that generating a statistically impressive graph did not automatically establish the underlying claim.

For example:

- A high R² in a log-log rank-frequency plot did not necessarily establish a perfect power law.
- A coloured heatmap could represent negative correlation, positive correlation, frequency or statistical distance depending on its colour scale.
- A strong ingredient co-occurrence edge did not necessarily indicate flavour compatibility.
- A network containing only the most frequent ingredients could hide the structure of the full ingredient vocabulary.
- Logarithmic transformations could improve visualization without changing rank-based correlations.
- A visually clear cluster could still have very weak quantitative separation.

We therefore revisited the original results and extended the analysis in a more critical and statistically controlled direction.

The modified report preserves the original 39 pages and appends 27 pages of new analysis, numbered from 40 to 66.

The additional work was organised around seven major refinement areas.

### 2.2 Reconstructed Normalization Procedure

The new normalization procedure involved:

1. Splitting ingredient strings on the pipe delimiter.
2. Applying Unicode NFKC normalization.
3. Converting text to lowercase.
4. Normalizing whitespace.
5. Removing boundary quantities and measurement units.
6. Repeating boundary removal up to three times.
7. Mapping Cointreau to orange liqueur.
8. Mapping Kahlua to coffee liqueur.
9. Retaining internal brands and descriptors.
10. Counting each normalized ingredient at most once per recipe.

We retained distinctions such as lime versus lime juice and fresh versus non-fresh forms in the reconstructed standard representation.

We also introduced a literal-string sensitivity representation in which quantity-bearing ingredient descriptions were not merged.

This was important because the results of Zipf, Heaps and co-occurrence analysis could change depending on the ingredient identity definition.

### 2.3 Why This Audit Was Necessary

Ingredient normalization affects nearly every subsequent result.

Changing ingredient identity changes:

- Vocabulary size.
- Ingredient frequency.
- Rank assignment.
- Recipe-size calculation.
- Information content.
- Heaps exponent.
- Co-occurrence counts.
- Network structure.

Therefore, we explicitly documented the reconstruction rather than treating the old and new ingredient counts as interchangeable.

## 3. Modification 1 – Testing Zipf's Law Across Multiple Rank Intervals

### 3.1 Problem in the Original Report

The original report primarily presented the Zipf rank-frequency fit over ranks 5–500.

The log-log graph showed a high R², but the empirical curve exhibited curvature.

This raised the question of whether the estimated exponent was stable across a wider rank range.

### 3.2 New Implementation

We fitted the rank-frequency relationship over three cumulative rank intervals:

- 5–500.
- 5–1000.
- 5–2000.

The model remained:

log10 f(r) = a - α × log10 r

The frequency definition was unchanged: the number of distinct recipes containing an ingredient.

The three intervals were overlapping cumulative windows, not independent segments.

### 3.3 Results

| Rank interval | Points | Alpha | R² |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 5–500 | 496 | 1.032 | 0.989 |
| 5–1000 | 996 | 1.123 | 0.989 |
| 5–2000 | 1996 | 1.187 | 0.991 |

The exponent increased as more of the lower-frequency ingredient tail was included.

This demonstrated that the fitted slope was not constant across the selected ranges.

### 3.4 Additional Diagnostics

We extended the analysis further by:

- Examining residual curvature.
- Comparing a free-slope model against a fixed inverse-rank slope.
- Fitting a quadratic relationship in log-rank space.
- Examining the influence of quantity-bearing literal strings.
- Separating the rank intervals into disjoint segments.
- Performing 250 recipe-resampling repetitions.

The literal-string representation produced much smaller slopes:

| Rank interval | Literal-string alpha |
|---|---:|
| 5–500 | 0.618 |
| 5–1000 | 0.683 |
| 5–2000 | 0.750 |

This showed that ingredient normalization materially influenced the scaling result.

The residual analysis also indicated systematic curvature rather than purely random deviations from a straight line.

### 3.5 Interpretation

The analysis supported the existence of strong rank-ordered ingredient reuse.

However, it did not establish one invariant exponent across all scales.

The most appropriate conclusion was that CocktailDB displayed approximate Zipf-like structure over the examined ranges, but the exact slope depended on rank interval and ingredient representation.

We also reinforced an important statistical distinction:

A high R² from log-log OLS is a descriptive fit, not a complete power-law validation procedure.

The Clauset-style frequency-distribution MLE and the rank-frequency OLS exponent measure different things and should not be confused.

## 4. Modification 2 – More Detailed Heaps' Law Visualization

### 4.1 Problem in the Original Report

The original Heaps analysis displayed:

- Mean vocabulary-growth curves.
- A 95% permutation band.
- A histogram of fitted beta exponents.

However, the distribution of vocabulary sizes at particular recipe counts was not directly visualized using boxplots.

This made it difficult to communicate how much vocabulary growth varied across the 100 random recipe orderings.

### 4.2 New Implementation

We generated vocabulary-growth distributions at fixed recipe counts.

The selected checkpoints included:

20, 100, 500, 1,000, 5,000, 10,000 and 16,872 recipes.

For each checkpoint, we calculated vocabulary size across the 100 random recipe orderings.

We also generated a separate boxplot of the fitted Heaps exponents.

### 4.3 Results

The reconstructed Heaps analysis produced:

- Mean beta: 0.757.
- Standard deviation: 0.010.
- Central 95% ordering range: [0.736, 0.773].

At the complete corpus size, every ordering necessarily contained the same final set of ingredients.

Therefore, the boxplot at R = 16,872 collapsed to one value: 13,860.

This was an important mathematical consistency check.

### 4.4 Interpretation

The analysis confirmed sublinear vocabulary growth over the fitted range.

The random permutations quantified variability caused by recipe ordering within the fixed dataset.

They did not measure uncertainty about every possible cocktail recipe or establish that the same exponent would apply to all cocktail collections.

The new mean beta of 0.757 was not directly interchangeable with the earlier 0.738 estimate because the reconstructed ingredient vocabulary and fitting grid differed.

## 5. Modification 3 – Correct Interpretation of Heatmaps and Correlation Results

### 5.1 Problem in the Original Report

The original report contained several heatmaps:

- Ingredient co-occurrence counts.
- Nutrient Spearman correlations.
- Distribution-fitting KS distances.

These represented different statistical quantities.

Their colours could easily be misinterpreted if the corresponding numerical scales were not examined.

We therefore revised the heatmap interpretation and added numerical annotations.

### 5.2 New Correlation Matrix

We generated an annotated Spearman correlation matrix with:

- A fixed colour range from -1 to +1.
- Zero positioned at the centre.
- Negative and positive associations represented using a diverging colour scale.
- Pairwise observation counts displayed in the cells.

The interpretation was clarified:

- Blue: negative association.
- White: association close to zero.
- Red: positive association.

This colour convention applied to the new diverging correlation matrix.

It did not apply to the original ingredient co-occurrence heatmap, where darker blue represented a higher shared-recipe count.

Similarly, the KS-distance heatmap represented distribution-fit distances, where smaller values indicated closer fits rather than weaker correlations.

### 5.3 Strong Nutrient Correlations

The reanalysis revealed several strong nutrient associations.

| Nutrient pair | Spearman correlation |
|---|---:|
| Carbohydrates vs sugar | 0.957 |
| Fat vs saturated fat | 0.916 |
| Calories vs carbohydrates | 0.696 |

The strong relationships were partly explained by the mathematical definitions of the variables.

For example:

- Sugar contributes to total carbohydrate mass.
- Saturated fat is part of total fat.
- Calories and macronutrients may increase together.

These are not evidence that one nutrient causes another.

### 5.4 Weak Associations and Their Interpretation

We revisited several weak or moderate relationships.

| Variable pair | n | Spearman rho |
|---|---:|---:|
| Ingredient count vs preparation time | 6,976 | 0.352 |
| Preparation time vs votes | 6,082 | -0.291 |
| Rating vs votes | 7,878 | -0.058 |
| Ingredient count vs calories | 5,683 | 0.247 |
| Ingredient count vs votes | 7,878 | -0.191 |
| Ingredient count vs protein | 4,988 | 0.144 |

We examined possible explanations.

**Bounded ratings:** 84.7% of analyzed ratings were at least four stars, limiting variation.

**Tied preparation times:** 69.7% of observed preparation times were exactly three minutes, restricting rank resolution.

**Ingredient-count limitations:** Every ingredient contributed equally to ingredient count, regardless of its quantity or nutritional contribution.

**Source heterogeneity:** The rating-vote relationship differed substantially between nutrient-format subsets.

The correlation between rating and votes was close to zero in colon-style nutrient records but more negative in compact-format and nutrient-absent subsets.

These formats were treated as source-format proxies, not confirmed source identities.

### 5.5 Important Statistical Understanding

A very small p-value does not necessarily imply a meaningful relationship.

For example, rating versus votes had a statistically detectable p-value, but the Spearman coefficient was only approximately -0.058.

We therefore placed more emphasis on effect size, confidence intervals and sample size than on statistical significance alone.

This allowed zero values to be retained.

We applied the transformation selectively to:

- Calories.
- Carbohydrates.

## 7. Modification 5 – Hierarchical Clustering of Ingredient Co-occurrence

### 7.1 Problem in the Original Report

The first report contained a co-occurrence heatmap and a thresholded PMI network.

However, ingredients were primarily displayed according to frequency, and there was no explicit hierarchical clustering of their recipe-membership patterns.

We therefore investigated whether ingredients could be grouped according to how similarly they appeared across recipes.

### 7.2 Jaccard Similarity

For two ingredients i and j, Jaccard similarity was calculated as:

J(i,j) = c(i,j) / [f(i) + f(j) - c(i,j)]

Where:

- c(i,j) is the number of recipes containing both ingredients.
- f(i) is the number of recipes containing ingredient i.
- f(j) is the number of recipes containing ingredient j.

The corresponding distance was:

D(i,j) = 1 - J(i,j)

This measures the overlap between recipe sets and reduces the automatic advantage of ingredients that are globally common.

### 7.3 Hierarchical Clustering

We selected the top 40 reconstructed ingredients by incidence.

We then:

1. Constructed recipe-membership representations.
2. Calculated the Jaccard similarity matrix.
3. Converted similarity into distance.
4. Applied average-linkage hierarchical clustering.
5. Generated a heatmap with dendrograms.

The resulting hierarchy was exploratory.

### 7.4 Cluster Evaluation

We examined cluster cuts for k = 2 through 8 using silhouette scores on the precomputed Jaccard distance.

The maximum silhouette score was only 0.020 at k = 3.

We used k = 3 to maintain consistent visual grouping, but the near-zero silhouette demonstrated that the global separation was weak.

### 7.5 Observed Groups

The three groups contained different co-use patterns.

**Group 1 – Teal**

Contained ingredients such as:

- Fresh lime juice.
- Angostura bitters.
- Fresh lemon juice.
- Sweet vermouth.
- Sugar syrup.
- Soda water.
- Dry vermouth.
- Orange bitters.
- Bourbon.

**Group 2 – Amber**

Contained a broader mixture of:

- Vodka.
- Lime juice.
- Lemon juice.
- Orange juice.
- Gin.
- Simple syrup.
- Pineapple juice.
- Triple sec.
- Ice.
- Tequila.

**Group 3 – Purple**

Contained:

- Coffee liqueur.

- Milk.

### 7.6 Interpretation and Limitations

Some familiar combinations appeared in the hierarchy:

- Triple sec and tequila.
- Coffee liqueur and Irish cream.
- Pineapple juice and coconut rum.
- Lemon juice and simple syrup.

However, the clustering did not establish taste compatibility, ingredient function or cultural origin.

The weak silhouette indicated that these were exploratory co-use groups, not validated cocktail categories.

## 8. Modification 6 – More Rigorous Ingredient Network Visualization

### 8.1 Problem in the Original Network

The original network used the top 40 ingredients and a minimum joint occurrence threshold.

The report had not fully quantified how edge widths were mapped to frequency or clearly separated frequency from association strength.

We therefore refined the network visualization.

### 8.2 Edge-Width Definition

For a pair of ingredients, normalized prevalence was defined as:

Prevalence(i,j) = c(i,j) / N

Where N was the full frequency-corpus size of 16,872 recipes.

Edge width was scaled relative to the maximum pair prevalence.

Node size was scaled according to ingredient frequency.

Node colours represented the three exploratory hierarchical co-use groups.

The positions were generated using a fixed seeded spring layout.

Spatial distance was not interpreted as a statistical similarity measurement.

### 8.3 Frequency, Jaccard, PMI and Disparity

We explicitly separated four quantities.

**Corpus prevalence**

c(i,j) / N

Measures how common a pair is in the entire corpus.

**Jaccard similarity**

c(i,j) / [f(i) + f(j) - c(i,j)]

Measures the overlap between the recipes containing the two ingredients.

**PMI**

ln[c(i,j)N / (f(i)f(j))]

Measures whether the pair occurs more frequently than expected from independent marginal prevalences.

**Disparity local share**

c(i,j) / sum_k c(i,k)

Measures how much of one ingredient's total pair weight is assigned to a particular neighbour.

These metrics answer different questions.

For example, vodka and orange juice appeared together in 320 recipes, while Irish cream and whipped cream appeared together in 72.

The former had greater corpus prevalence, while the latter had a higher PMI.

Therefore, the most common pair was not necessarily the most disproportionately associated pair.

### 8.4 Threshold Clarification

The original report contained two descriptions of the network threshold:

- Positive PMI.
- PMI greater than 0.4.

These are not equivalent.

The reconstructed network explicitly used:

- Minimum co-occurrence count: 25.
- Natural-log PMI greater than 0.4.

The resulting network contained 159 edges rather than the original 166, partly because ingredient normalization had changed.

This made the selection rule more explicit and reproducible.

## 9. Modification 7 – Full Ingredient Network and Disparity-Filter Backbone

This was the most substantial new extension beyond the original report.

### 9.1 Motivation

The original network displayed only a selected subset of common ingredients.

It could not show how the entire ingredient vocabulary was connected or how much information was lost when filtering the network.

We therefore constructed the full ingredient co-occurrence graph before applying top-N selection or PMI thresholds.

### 9.2 Full Network Construction

Every distinct ingredient was represented as a node.

For each recipe, every unordered pair of distinct ingredients contributed one unit of co-occurrence weight.

We built two network representations:

1. Boundary-normalized ingredient strings.
2. Literal quantity-bearing strings.

The resulting full graphs were:

| Quantity | Boundary-normalized | Literal strings |
|---|---:|---:|
| Nodes | 13,860 | 31,224 |
| Observed pair edges | 99,039 | 152,293 |
| Pair-incidence weight sum | 172,300 | 173,077 |

The two representations showed how ingredient normalization affected the topology of the graph.

### 9.3 Disparity Filter

We studied the disparity-filter method from Serrano, Boguñá and Vespignani (2009).

For an edge between nodes i and j:

- w(i,j) represents edge weight.
- s(i) represents the total strength of node i.
- k(i) represents the degree of node i.
- p(i,j) represents the fraction of node i's strength assigned to j.

The normalized local share was:

p(i,j) = w(i,j) / s(i)

The endpoint significance expression was:

a(i,j) = [1 - p(i,j)]^[k(i)-1]

We used the rule that an undirected edge was retained when either endpoint passed the selected alpha threshold.

This was the OR rule.

We also evaluated an AND-rule sensitivity analysis, which required significance at both endpoints.

### 9.4 Alpha Sensitivity Analysis

We examined multiple alpha values:

0, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 0.9 and 1.

Selected results:

| Alpha | Retained edges | Active nodes | Retained weight |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.001 | 895 | 332 | 18.79% |
| 0.005 | 1,432 | 469 | 22.84% |
| 0.01 | 1,766 | 541 | 24.75% |
| 0.025 | 2,478 | 670 | 28.00% |
| 0.05 | 3,500 | 860 | 31.53% |
| 0.1 | 5,433 | 1,259 | 36.44% |
| 0.2 | 9,873 | 2,250 | 43.61% |
| 0.5 | 88,751 | 13,229 | 93.99% |
| 0.9 | 99,039 | 13,860 | 100% |

At alpha = 0.05:

- Only 3.53% of edges survived.
- Only 6.20% of nodes remained active.
- 31.53% of pair-incidence weight was retained.

This demonstrated that a readable network backbone could represent a compact recurring core while excluding most of the rare ingredient vocabulary.

### 9.5 Important Observation

A sharp transition occurred between alpha = 0.2 and alpha = 0.5.

Edge retention increased from approximately 9.97% to 89.61%.

This was associated with the large number of low-count edges having similar local weights.

We also observed that the full network had 128 connected components, with the largest containing 13,421 nodes.

High edge retention therefore did not automatically imply a single universally connected network.

### 9.6 Retained Versus Non-Retained Edges

We compared retained and non-retained ties.

Some examples:

| Ingredient pair | Joint recipes | Minimum endpoint p-value | Result at 0.05 |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Fresh lemon juice + dark rum | 7 | 0.0387 | Retained |
| Rum + soda water | 7 | 0.0430 | Retained |
| Coconut rum + Irish cream | 14 | 0.0524 | Not retained |
| Melon liqueur + almond liqueur | 12 | 0.0647 | Not retained |

This showed that the disparity filter did not simply retain the pairs with the highest raw counts.

The local importance of an edge relative to the other connections of its endpoint mattered.

### 9.7 Comparison Against a Global Count-Based Filter

We compared the disparity filter at alpha = 0.05 against a simple baseline retaining the same number of highest-count edges.

| Method | Edges | Active nodes | Retained weight |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Local disparity filter | 3,500 | 860 | 31.53% |
| Largest-count baseline | 3,500 | 577 | 32.20% |

The global baseline retained slightly more total weight but reached fewer nodes.

The local filter therefore provided broader coverage across lower-strength regions, although it was not superior on every criterion.

### 9.8 Statistical Robustness

We extended the backbone analysis using:

- OR versus AND endpoint rules.
- Boundary-normalized versus literal-string representations.
- Multiple alpha thresholds.
- Benjamini–Hochberg multiple-testing sensitivity.
- Fisher exact tests under an ingredient-occurrence independence null.

At alpha = 0.05:

- The normalized OR rule retained 3,500 edges.
- The normalized AND rule retained 1,676 edges.
- Benjamini–Hochberg sensitivity retained 628 edges.

We also performed one-sided Fisher exact tests on all 780 top-40 ingredient pairs, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

The comparison demonstrated that passing the disparity filter was not equivalent to showing positive ingredient-pair enrichment under an independence null.

For example, vodka and lemon juice occurred together 173 times and passed the disparity filter, but had negative PMI and a Fisher enrichment q-value of 1.

Therefore, local network significance could not be interpreted as evidence of positive pairing preference.

### 9.9 Limitations of the Backbone

The disparity filter uses a local strength-allocation null.

A retained edge is not proven to represent a true culinary relationship, and a non-retained edge is not necessarily unimportant.

The endpoint tests are dependent because many edges share ingredients and recipes.

The unadjusted alpha sweep was exploratory.

Even the alpha = 0.05 backbone retained only a small fraction of the original ingredient vocabulary.

A stronger future null model would preserve recipe-size and ingredient-degree characteristics through recipe-incidence randomization.

## 10. Additional PCA Sensitivity Analysis

The original PCA used standardized nutrient values on their original scales.

However, extremely large nutrient observations could influence the covariance structure.

We therefore repeated PCA using log1p-transformed nutrient inputs.

The comparison used exactly the same:

- 2,319 complete-case recipes.
- Eight nutrient variables.
- Standardization procedure.

### Results

| PCA input | PC1 | PC2 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Original nutrient values | 61.46% | 16.83% |
| Log1p nutrient values | 59.30% | 17.74% |

The first two components continued to explain a substantial proportion of the variance.

The loadings changed after transformation, with sodium's PC1 loading increasing from approximately 0.084 to 0.329.

This demonstrated that extreme nutrient values influenced the original PCA geometry.

We concluded that PCA components were descriptive representations of reported nutrient quantities, not universal health scores or validated recipe clusters.

## 11. Mathematical and Interpretational Corrections

During the modification stage, we also revisited the original report for inconsistencies.

Several corrections were documented.

### 11.1 Rating-Vote Correlation Sign

The original Figure 9 caption described the association as positive while reporting a Spearman correlation of -0.058.

The correct interpretation is a weak negative association.

### 11.2 Ratings Near Five Stars

The earlier description of ratings as left-censored toward five stars was too strong.

The distribution demonstrated a ceiling concentration near the upper end of the rating scale, but the dataset did not establish a formal censoring mechanism.

### 11.3 Heaps Exponent Comparison

The earlier report described beta approximately 0.74 as close to the reference paper's approximately 0.56.

Both indicate sublinear growth, but numerical closeness should not be asserted without an agreed comparison criterion.

### 11.4 Menzerath–Altmann Equation Check

We revisited the fitted function:

y(L) = a × L^b × exp(cL)

The derivative of its logarithm is:

d(log y)/dL = b/L + c

A positive-L minimum requires the appropriate sign combination of b and c.

The quoted reference parameters b = -0.26 and c = -0.019 cannot produce a positive-L minimum under this stated form.

For the cocktail fit, b = -0.157 and c = 0.042 imply a model minimum near L = 3.74.

We recorded this as an internal equation and parameter-transcription check.

The reference paper's reported parameter values should be verified before repeating the comparison with its claimed minimum near L = 13.

### 11.5 PMI Threshold Consistency

The original report used both positive PMI and PMI greater than 0.4 in different descriptions.

These represent different edge-selection criteria.

The modified report made the actual threshold explicit.

### 11.6 Distribution-Fitting Interpretation

We also clarified that:

- A high fitting score is not automatically a goodness-of-fit test.
- A visually symmetric transformed distribution does not establish log-normality.
- Candidate KS rankings are descriptive unless the fitting and testing procedure is properly calibrated.

These corrections were important because we wanted the final report to communicate what the analysis actually established, rather than what a graph might appear to suggest.

## 13. Main Limitations That Remained After Modification

Although the new analysis improved the original report, several limitations remained.

### 13.1 Ingredient Ontology

Ingredient normalization was still partly rule-based.

Brands, ingredient states, quantities and preparation descriptions could remain fragmented.

The reconstructed standard vocabulary was not identical to the earlier implementation.

### 13.2 Missing Data

Cuisine, category, nutrient, ratings and preparation-time information remained incomplete.

The new analyses did not remove the underlying selection bias introduced by missingness.

### 13.3 CocktailDB Representativeness

The dataset was still a relatively small, online, cocktail-focused corpus.

Its results could not establish universal properties of all cocktails or traditional cuisines.

### 13.4 Network Null Model

The disparity filter was based on a local strength-allocation null.

It did not directly test ingredient independence across recipes.

The Fisher exact comparison used a different null and also had assumptions concerning exchangeability of recipes.

A size- and degree-preserving recipe-incidence randomization was identified as a stronger future analysis.

### 13.5 Network Threshold Sensitivity

The retained backbone depended strongly on alpha.

At alpha = 0.05, most ingredient nodes and edges were removed.

Therefore, a filtered network could not be treated as a complete representation of culinary diversity.

### 13.6 Hierarchical Clustering

The maximum silhouette score was only 0.020.

The groups were exploratory co-use patterns rather than statistically well-separated cocktail categories.

### 13.7 Nutrient and Serving Information

Serving-size standardization remained unavailable.

Consequently, the extreme values could not be reliably interpreted as per-glass nutritional values.

### 13.8 No Direct Flavour Chemistry

The ingredient networks were built from recipe co-occurrence.

They did not include shared flavour molecules or chemical compatibility information.

Linking CocktailDB with flavour-compound information remained an important future direction.

## 14. Final Outcome of the Modification Stage

The second report substantially improved the scientific interpretation of the original analysis.

The modifications did not simply add more graphs. They challenged the original assumptions and tested how sensitive the results were to methodological choices.

The major conclusions were:

**Zipf:** Ingredient usage was strongly concentrated and approximately Zipf-like, but the estimated exponent changed across rank intervals and normalization levels. A single invariant exponent was not supported globally.

**Heaps:** Vocabulary growth remained sublinear. The reconstructed mean beta was approximately 0.757, with variability quantified across 100 recipe orderings.

**Menzerath–Altmann:** The cocktail dataset did not provide the same recipe-size range as the global food corpus, and the reported relationship required careful interpretation.

**Nutrient distributions:** Log1p transformations improved visualization, but the nutrient distributions remained heterogeneous. The best-fitting distribution differed by nutrient.

**Correlation analysis:** The new numerical heatmaps revealed strong nutrient associations and clarified why some relationships remained weak. Effect sizes and observation counts were more informative than colour alone.

**Hierarchical clustering:** Jaccard-based clustering provided exploratory ingredient co-use groups, but the low silhouette score did not support strongly separated global clusters.

**Network backbone:** The disparity filter extracted a compact recurring ingredient network, but it also removed most of the original vocabulary at conventional thresholds.

**Statistical robustness:** The OR/AND comparison, multiple-testing sensitivity and Fisher exact tests demonstrated that local network significance was not equivalent to positive ingredient-pair enrichment.

Overall, this phase moved our work from exploratory implementation towards critical statistical validation.

The most important improvement was that we began explicitly testing whether the conclusions remained valid when rank ranges, ingredient definitions, transformations, clustering methods and network thresholds changed.

## 15. Planned Work for the Next Meeting

After completing the modifications, the following research directions remained important.

### 15.1 Improve Ingredient Entity Resolution

- Develop a more systematic cocktail-specific ingredient ontology.
- Identify brands, aliases and canonical ingredient names.
- Separate quantities and units from ingredient identity.
- Preserve meaningful ingredient states and preparation descriptions.
- Evaluate a cocktail-specific NER pipeline.

### 15.2 Strengthen the Network Null Model

- Implement recipe-incidence randomization.
- Preserve recipe-size distributions.
- Preserve ingredient frequencies where appropriate.
- Compare randomized network statistics with observed network structure.
- Investigate whether ingredient-pair associations remain significant after controlling for these structural constraints.

### 15.3 Investigate Flavour Chemistry

- Explore linking normalized ingredients with FlavorDB or equivalent flavour-compound resources.
- Compare recipe co-occurrence networks with shared-compound networks.
- Investigate whether frequently co-used cocktail ingredients also exhibit chemical similarity.
- Distinguish chemical similarity from cultural or functional pairing.

### 15.4 Extend the Nutrient Analysis

- Investigate serving-size standardization.
- Separate active preparation time from infusion or elapsed time where possible.
- Validate extreme nutrient observations against their original sources.
- Re-examine distribution fitting after improving data quality.

### 15.5 Test Generality Across Additional Corpora

- Compare CocktailDB with a larger independent cocktail corpus.
- Investigate whether Zipf and Heaps exponents remain stable.
- Examine whether ingredient diversity growth is domain-specific.
- Consider historical menus to distinguish traditional recipes from modern variations.

### 15.6 Strengthen Statistical Reporting

- Continue reporting effect sizes, confidence intervals and sample sizes.
- Document all normalization choices.
- Separate exploratory visual patterns from validated statistical conclusions.
- Avoid interpreting correlations as causal relationships.
- Preserve sensitivity analyses alongside primary estimates.

## 16. Overall Research Reflection

The modification stage was an important step in improving the quality of our computational gastronomy research.

The first report demonstrated that the statistical framework of *Universal Statistical Laws Governing Culinary Design* could be adapted to CocktailDB.

The second report investigated whether those initial results were stable, whether the graphs were being interpreted correctly and whether additional network methods could reveal more about ingredient composition.

We learned that statistical regularities should not be accepted solely because a graph looks convincing.

A high R² can coexist with systematic curvature. A small p-value can accompany a negligible effect size. A network backbone can look meaningful while retaining only a small fraction of the vocabulary. A cluster can be visually distinct while having a near-zero silhouette score.

The new analyses made these distinctions explicit.

The resulting research contribution was therefore not simply that CocktailDB reproduced the paper's laws.

It was that we systematically investigated which aspects of the original framework transferred to a specialised cocktail corpus, which differed, and which could not be established using the available data.

This provided a more defensible basis for the next stage of research, particularly ingredient ontology development, flavour-chemistry integration and statistically controlled network analysis.
