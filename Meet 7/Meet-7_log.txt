### 6.3 Vote-Adjusted Rating

One of the important feature-engineering steps involved adjusting recipe ratings based on the number of votes received.

We noticed that recipes with very few votes could have extreme ratings. For example, a recipe with a single vote could receive a rating of 5.0 even though the evidence supporting that rating was limited.

To address this issue, we implemented a Bayesian-style weighted rating:

`adjusted_rating = (V * R + m * C) / (V + m)`

Where:

- `V` represents the recipe's vote count.
- `R` represents its original rating.
- `C` represents the global mean rating.
- `m` represents the smoothing parameter, set to 10.

The global mean acted as a prior estimate, while the recipe's own rating contributed according to the number of votes it had received.

**Why we used this:**

A recipe with many votes should generally provide stronger evidence of its rating than a recipe with only one or two votes.

The adjustment therefore reduced the influence of extreme ratings based on very small sample sizes.

We used the adjusted ratings to rank recipes and identify the top ten recipes by vote-adjusted rating.

This introduced us to the idea that a numerical observation should sometimes be interpreted in the context of the amount of evidence supporting it.

## 9. Limitations and Problems Identified

The initial analysis also exposed several limitations in both the dataset and our methodology.

We documented these because they would directly influence the reliability of any future statistical or computational study.

### 9.1 Extensive Missing Values

A substantial portion of the dataset lacked nutritional, preparation-time, rating, vote and category information.

This restricted the number of observations available for different analyses.

More importantly, the observations available for one analysis were not necessarily the same observations available for another.

For example, nutritional analysis would have been based on a much smaller subset than ingredient-frequency analysis.

### 9.2 Cuisine Information Was Unusable

Cuisine was missing for more than 99% of the dataset.

Consequently, cuisine-based comparisons could not be performed reliably in this initial exercise.

We could not meaningfully investigate regional or national differences using this field.

### 9.3 Nutritional Analysis Was Not Completed

Nutrients contained approximately 66.3% missing values.

We excluded it from the main analysis rather than producing potentially misleading conclusions from incomplete information.

A separate nutrient-focused analysis on non-missing records remained a possible direction.

### 9.4 Ingredient Normalisation Was Incomplete

This was one of the most important methodological limitations.

We counted raw ingredient strings rather than canonical ingredients.

Quantity and measurement information remained embedded in the strings.

As a result:

- The same ingredient could appear under multiple textual representations.
- Ingredient frequencies could be fragmented.
- The top ingredient ranking could differ from a ranking based on canonical ingredient identities.
- Reliable ingredient co-occurrence analysis would require a normalisation pipeline.

A more complete approach would involve extracting ingredient names, quantities and units separately, followed by canonicalisation and alias resolution.

### 9.5 Preparation-Time Parsing Was Imperfect

The original preparation-time field contained inconsistent formats.

Regular expressions could extract many values, but some entries could not be parsed reliably.

Bare numerical values without recognisable units were treated as missing.

There was also a possibility that some unusually large values represented incorrect or inconsistently recorded information.

Therefore, parsing alone was not sufficient to guarantee that all preparation-time values were semantically valid.

### 9.6 Ratings Could Be Biased

Most available ratings were concentrated between 4.0 and 5.0.

This could reflect:

- Rating inflation.
- Selection bias.
- User participation patterns.
- Differences in the number of ratings received by individual recipes.

We could not treat the rating distribution as an unbiased measure of objective recipe quality.

### 9.7 Votes Were Highly Skewed

A small number of recipes received very large numbers of votes.

The mean vote count was therefore influenced by extreme observations.

Median and logarithmic visualisations were more informative than relying on the mean alone.

### 9.8 Vote-Adjusted Ratings Had Parameter Dependence

The adjusted rating used a smoothing parameter of 10.

Although this helped account for low vote counts, the final ranking depended on the selected parameter.

A more complete study could investigate sensitivity to different smoothing values and evaluate whether the rankings remained stable.

### 9.9 Category Sample-Size Imbalance

The 429 categories were not equally represented.

Categories with very few recipes could produce unstable mean ratings and preparation-time estimates.

The minimum five-recipe threshold used for category rating analysis reduced some of this issue but did not fully address statistical uncertainty.

### 9.10 Ingredient Count Was a Simplistic Complexity Measure

Ingredient count did not capture:

- Ingredient quantities.
- Relative proportions.
- Preparation methods.
- Ingredient interactions.
- Mixing or layering complexity.
- The order in which ingredients were added.

Two cocktails with the same number of ingredients could have very different compositions and preparation requirements.

### 9.11 Pearson Correlation Was Not Sufficient to Explain Relationships

The correlation analysis focused on linear relationships.

A low correlation did not rule out nonlinear dependencies or relationships that appeared only within particular categories.

We also did not perform causal analysis.

### 9.12 No Predictive or Unsupervised Modelling

This stage was intentionally limited to exploratory data analysis.

We did not implement:

- Rating prediction.
- Recommendation systems.
- Nutritional prediction.
- Recipe classification.
- Ingredient-based clustering.
- Cocktail similarity models.
- Statistical hypothesis testing.

The analysis was intended to establish an understanding of the data before deciding which computational methods would be meaningful.

### 9.13 No Ingredient Interaction or Culinary Compatibility Analysis

Although the dataset contained ingredient lists, the initial EDA did not establish whether ingredient combinations were statistically or chemically meaningful.

We did not investigate:

- Ingredient co-occurrence patterns.
- Pairwise ingredient associations.
- Higher-order ingredient combinations.
- Shared flavour compounds.
- Chemical flavour compatibility.
- Statistical laws governing ingredient selection.

These questions required a deeper theoretical and computational framework.
