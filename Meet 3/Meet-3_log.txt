## 2. Understanding the What's Cooking? Dataset

The first step was to inspect the dataset and understand its structure before beginning any analysis.

The dataset was obtained from the Kaggle What's Cooking? competition.

We used the training file, `train.json`, because it contained cuisine labels alongside recipe identifiers and ingredient lists.

Each record contained three primary fields:

- `id`: Unique identifier for a recipe.
- `cuisine`: Cuisine label associated with the recipe.
- `ingredients`: List of ingredients used in the recipe.

An example of the structure was:

```json
{
    "id": 10259,
    "cuisine": "greek",
    "ingredients": [
        "romaine lettuce",
        "black olives",
        "grape tomatoes",
        "garlic",
        "feta cheese"
    ]
}
```

This simple structure was sufficient for investigating recipe composition, ingredient popularity, cuisine-wise distributions and ingredient-category patterns.

### Dataset statistics

Our initial exploration established the following:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---:|
| Total recipes | 39,774 |
| Number of cuisines | 20 |
| Unique ingredients | 6,714 |
| Average recipe size | 10.77 |
| Most represented cuisine | Italian |
| Italian recipes | 7,838 |
| Least represented cuisine | Brazilian |
| Brazilian recipes | 467 |

We also observed that the dataset had an uneven distribution of recipes across cuisines.

Italian cuisine had substantially more observations than Brazilian cuisine, which became an important consideration when comparing the results across different regions.

### Initial preprocessing

We performed the following preprocessing operations:

1. Loaded the JSON records into a structured pandas DataFrame.
2. Examined the available columns and their data types.
3. Checked missing values and duplicate recipe identifiers.
4. Calculated recipe size by counting the number of ingredients in each recipe.
5. Created an ingredient set for every recipe to avoid counting repeated ingredients more than once within the same recipe.
6. Constructed ingredient-frequency statistics across the entire dataset.

The report records that no rows were removed after the missing-value and duplicate-ID checks.

An important distinction we established was between ingredient occurrences and recipe-level ingredient frequency.

For example, if salt appears multiple times in a single recipe's ingredient list, it should still contribute only one count to the number of recipes containing salt.

This is why we used sets for recipe-level ingredient counting.

## 3. Differences Between CulinaryDB and What's Cooking?

One of the most important parts of our presentation was explaining why the dataset used in our practical work was different from the dataset used in the original paper.

We compared both datasets to understand what could and could not be reproduced.

### A. Dataset-level comparison

| Parameter | Original CulinaryDB study | What's Cooking? |
|---|---|---|
| Total recipes | 45,772 | 39,774 |
| Geographical coverage | 22 geo-cultural regions | 20 cuisine labels |
| Unique ingredients | 707 mapped ingredients in the paper's dataset representation | 6,714 ingredient strings |
| Average recipe size | Approximately 9 | 10.77 |
| Recipe information | Recipe details, ingredients and cooking procedures | ID, cuisine and ingredient list |
| Ingredient categories | 21 categories | 16 rule-based categories in our analysis |
| Flavor molecule information | Integrated with FlavorDB | Not provided |
| Ingredient aliasing | Curated and standardised | No equivalent full aliasing pipeline |
| Cooking procedure information | Available in the original compiled resource | Not available in the training records |

### B. Difference in geographical representation

The original paper grouped recipes into 22 geo-cultural regions.

For example, it considered the Indian Subcontinent, Scandinavia, DACH countries, the British Isles and other geographical groupings.

What's Cooking? instead contains 20 cuisine labels, such as:

- Indian
- Italian
- Japanese
- Mexican
- Chinese
- Thai
- Greek
- Brazilian
- French

These labels are not equivalent to the 22 regions used in the paper.

For example, an Indian cuisine label cannot automatically be considered identical to the original paper's Indian Subcontinent region.

We therefore treated the two datasets as separate representations of culinary traditions rather than assuming that their regional classifications were interchangeable.

### C. Difference in ingredient representation

The original study performed extensive ingredient aliasing.

It standardised linguistic variations, synonyms, spelling differences and compound ingredients to establish a reliable mapping between recipe ingredients and FlavorDB.

In contrast, What's Cooking? provides ingredient names as strings.

For example, entries such as `garlic`, `garlic cloves` and `minced garlic` may be represented separately.

This means that the 6,714 unique ingredient strings in our dataset should not be interpreted as 6,714 chemically distinct natural ingredients.

We also recognised that the absence of a complete ingredient-aliasing process could affect the estimated ingredient diversity and frequency distributions.

### D. Difference in flavor information

This was the most significant limitation.

The original research connected ingredients to their chemical flavor molecules through FlavorDB.

This allowed the authors to calculate how many flavor compounds were shared between two ingredients.

What's Cooking? does not provide:

- Flavor molecule identifiers.
- Chemical compound profiles.
- Ingredient-to-flavor-molecule mappings.
- Cooking-induced chemical transformations.
- Ingredient quantities.

Consequently, we could analyse which ingredients occurred together but could not establish whether those ingredients shared flavor molecules.

We explicitly explained that ingredient co-occurrence and chemical flavor sharing are two different concepts.

Two ingredients frequently occurring together in recipes do not necessarily share chemical flavor compounds.

## 5. Detailed Exploratory Data Analysis

We then performed the analysis and generated the paper-inspired visualisations.

### Analysis 1 — Ingredient Category Composition by Cuisine

The original paper uses a heatmap to compare the ingredient-category composition of different world regions.

We recreated a similar analysis using the What's Cooking? dataset.

However, What's Cooking? does not provide official ingredient-category labels.

Therefore, we developed a rule-based categorisation approach.

We implemented an ingredient-category mapping function named:

`assign_ingredient_category()`

The function used keyword matching to assign each ingredient to one of 16 approximate categories:

1. Vegetable
2. Fruit
3. Meat
4. Seafood
5. Dairy
6. Legume
7. Grain/Cereal
8. Nut/Seed
9. Spice
10. Herb
11. Oil/Fat
12. Sauce/Condiment
13. Sweetener
14. Beverage
15. Bakery
16. Other

For every recipe, we identified which categories appeared at least once.

For every cuisine, we calculated the proportion of recipes containing each category.

The formula was:

\[
P(c,k)=
\frac{\text{Recipes in cuisine }c\text{ containing category }k}
{\text{Total recipes in cuisine }c}
\]

We then constructed a cuisine-by-category matrix using pandas and plotted it using Seaborn.

**Findings:**

- Vegetables, spices and sauces/condiments appeared frequently across cuisines.
- Different cuisines showed variations in their meat, seafood, dairy and legume composition.
- Normalisation allowed comparisons between cuisines despite differences in recipe counts.
- Our categories were approximate and dependent on the quality of the keyword-matching rules.

We explicitly acknowledged that the categories were our analytical approximation, not official labels from the dataset or an exact reproduction of the original paper's 21-category system.

### Analysis 2 — Recipe Size Distribution by Cuisine

The original paper examines recipe-size distributions across different world regions.

We reproduced a similar analysis.

We defined recipe size as the number of ingredients in a recipe.

For each cuisine, we calculated the frequency of recipes containing a particular number of ingredients.

We converted the frequencies into percentages to make the distributions comparable.

We also included a WORLD curve representing the complete dataset.

The individual cuisine curves were displayed as thin lines, while the WORLD curve was highlighted using a thicker black line.

We limited the horizontal axis to the 99th percentile for better visual readability.

**Findings:**

- Average recipe size across the dataset was approximately 10.77 ingredients.
- Most cuisines exhibited a unimodal distribution.
- Recipe sizes generally increased towards a peak and subsequently declined.
- Moroccan, Indian and Vietnamese cuisines tended to have larger average recipe sizes.

An important observation was that the original paper reported an average of approximately nine ingredients per recipe.

We discussed why our result differed.

The datasets were collected from different sources, contained different recipes and used different ingredient representations.

Therefore, the difference in average recipe size was not necessarily an inconsistency in the analysis.

### Analysis 3 — Ingredient Frequency-Rank Distribution

This analysis was inspired by Figure 3(b) of the original paper.

We wanted to understand whether a small number of ingredients dominated recipe composition across cuisines.

For each cuisine, we counted the number of recipes containing each ingredient.

We sorted the frequencies in descending order and assigned ranks to the ingredients.

We then plotted ingredient rank against frequency using logarithmic scales on both axes.

The WORLD curve represented the aggregate dataset.

**Findings:**

- A steep decrease in frequency was observed from the most popular ingredients towards lower-ranked ingredients.
- A small number of staple ingredients appeared in many recipes.
- Most ingredients appeared in relatively few recipes.
- Individual cuisines differed in how quickly ingredient frequency decreased with rank.

This demonstrated a head-heavy ingredient popularity distribution across the dataset.

### Analysis 4 — Number of Unique Ingredients by Cuisine

We investigated the ingredient vocabulary of each cuisine.

For every cuisine, we collected all ingredients occurring across its recipes and calculated the number of distinct ingredient strings.

The resulting values were sorted and plotted as a bar chart.

**Findings:**

- Italian cuisine had the largest ingredient vocabulary, with 2,929 unique ingredients.
- Mexican cuisine also had a relatively large ingredient vocabulary.
- Smaller cuisines still exhibited considerable ingredient diversity.

We discussed why this metric should not be confused with the actual culinary diversity of a country.

The number of unique ingredient strings is influenced by the number of recipes, ingredient naming conventions and the level of standardisation.

It measures the breadth of the dataset's vocabulary rather than the completeness of a cuisine's real-world culinary traditions.

### Analysis 5 — Number of Recipes by Cuisine

## 7. Which Parts of the Original Paper Could We Reproduce?

An important part of our work was determining the boundary between a paper-inspired reproduction and a genuine reproduction of the original experiments.

We classified the original analyses into two groups.

### Analyses we successfully reproduced in an adapted form

| Original paper analysis | Our implementation |
|---|---|
| Ingredient-category composition | Rule-based category heatmap |
| Recipe-size distribution | Cuisine-wise percentage distributions |
| Ingredient popularity | Rank-frequency curves |
| Regional/cuisine comparisons | Cuisine-wise descriptive analysis |
| Ingredient popularity summaries | Overall and cuisine-specific top ingredients |

These analyses used the recipe and ingredient information available in What's Cooking?.

### Analyses that could not be reproduced

**1. Chemical food-pairing scores**

The original paper calculates the overlap of flavor molecules between ingredient pairs.

We could not perform this because What's Cooking? does not contain FlavorDB mappings or chemical compound information.

**2. Randomised cuisine experiments**

The original paper compares actual cuisine compositions with several randomised controls.

Although recipe-level randomisation could be implemented, reproducing the paper's food-pairing experiments requires flavor profiles for the ingredients.

We therefore did not claim to reproduce those experiments.

**3. Z-score analysis**

The original paper uses Z-scores to measure how significantly the observed food-pairing patterns deviate from randomised counterparts.

Without the corresponding flavor-sharing scores, we could not calculate comparable Z-scores.

**4. Ingredient contribution analysis**

The original paper measures the change in food-pairing scores after removing ingredients.

We could not reproduce this chemical food-pairing contribution analysis without the flavor-molecule data.

We deliberately avoided inventing flavor-pairing scores, substituting arbitrary ingredient similarities or presenting unsupported Z-scores.

This was an important methodological decision that we explained in our presentation.

## 8. Presentation to the Professor and Discussion

After completing the analysis, we presented our notebook results and the final report to the professor.

We explained the complete workflow, starting from the dataset structure and preprocessing, followed by the descriptive analysis, visualisations, findings and limitations.

We also explained the reasoning behind choosing What's Cooking? and how its structure differed from the original CulinaryDB dataset.

### Questions and clarifications addressed during the discussion

**A. Why did we use a different dataset from the original paper?**

We explained that the provided What's Cooking? dataset offered structured recipe-level information, including cuisine labels and ingredient lists.

It was sufficient for studying recipe sizes, ingredient popularity, category composition and cuisine-wise differences.

However, it did not provide the complete flavor-molecule information needed for the original chemical food-pairing methodology.

**B. Why was the average recipe size different from the original paper?**

We explained that the original paper and our analysis used different recipe collections.

Their sources, ingredient representations and recipe distributions were different.

Therefore, the average number of ingredients was expected to vary.

**C. Why did we use percentages for the category heatmap?**

The number of recipes varied considerably across cuisines.

Comparing raw category counts would have favoured cuisines with more recipes.

By dividing the number of recipes containing a category by the total number of recipes in that cuisine, we obtained comparable proportions.

**D. Why did we use sets instead of directly counting ingredient strings?**

We wanted to measure the number of recipes containing an ingredient.

If an ingredient occurred more than once in a recipe, counting every occurrence would artificially increase its frequency.

Using sets ensured one contribution per ingredient per recipe.

**E. Why did we use a rule-based ingredient categorisation system?**

The dataset did not provide official ingredient categories.

We therefore developed keyword-based categorisation to obtain an approximate category-level representation.

We also acknowledged that this approach could misclassify ambiguous ingredients and would benefit from a more comprehensive ingredient ontology.

**F. Does the number of unique ingredients indicate culinary diversity?**

We clarified that it indicates the vocabulary represented in the dataset.

It does not necessarily indicate the actual diversity of a cuisine because recipe counts, data sources, naming variations and preprocessing influence the result.

**G. Can ingredient co-occurrence establish food pairing?**

We discussed that ingredients occurring together in recipes demonstrate a culinary association, not necessarily chemical flavor compatibility.

The original paper's methodology is based on flavor-molecule overlap, which is a different and more specific measurement.

**H. Why did we not reproduce the randomised models and Z-scores?**

We explained that those models were designed to compare flavor-sharing patterns.

Without ingredient-to-molecule mappings, the necessary food-pairing scores could not be computed.

We chose to document the limitation rather than report results that the available data could not support.

Through this discussion, we clarified the assumptions behind our analyses, the meaning of our statistical measures and the limitations of the dataset.

## 9. Limitations Identified During the Work

Our practical work also helped us identify several limitations that could affect the interpretation of the results.

### Dataset limitations

1. The dataset contains only 20 cuisine labels, which are not directly equivalent to the 22 geographical regions of the original study.
2. The recipe count is unevenly distributed across cuisines.
3. Ingredient names are not fully standardised.
4. The dataset does not provide quantities for ingredients.
5. Cooking procedures and preparation methods are unavailable in the training records.
6. Flavor molecule information is absent.
7. Some recipes may contain ingredients that are represented using multiple names.

### Methodological limitations

1. Our 16-category classification is based on keyword rules and is not an official taxonomy.
2. Ingredient frequency can be influenced by repeated staples and common recipe-writing conventions.
3. Unique ingredient counts depend on naming consistency.
4. Mean recipe size does not fully describe the distribution of recipe complexity.
5. Descriptive patterns do not establish causal relationships.
6. Our analyses do not establish whether ingredients have similar chemical flavor profiles.

We also recognised that the dataset's class imbalance could affect comparisons and that a more sophisticated ingredient standardisation process would be necessary for more rigorous cross-cuisine analysis.
