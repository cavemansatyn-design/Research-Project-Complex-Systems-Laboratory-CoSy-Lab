## 1. Research Background and Objectives

Following our study of *Data-Driven Investigations of Culinary Patterns in Traditional Recipes Across the World* and *Hierarchical Clustering of World Cuisines*, we undertook a comprehensive exploratory data analysis and computational investigation using the original CulinaryDB dataset.

The objective was to understand the structure of CulinaryDB, examine ingredient distributions across world cuisines, reproduce relevant analytical patterns from the research literature, and implement ingredient-based hierarchical clustering methods to investigate similarities between cuisines.

The work was organised around three major objectives:

1. Conduct descriptive exploratory data analysis of CulinaryDB, examining recipe characteristics, ingredient popularity, category composition, regional distributions and dataset provenance.
2. Implement frequent ingredient-pattern-based hierarchical clustering and authenticity-based clustering to investigate relationships between cuisines.
3. Compare different distance metrics, examine the resulting dendrograms and investigate whether the computationally identified relationships were consistent with known geographical and historical culinary connections.

The analysis was conducted directly on CulinaryDB, using the methodologies discussed in the research papers as its academic and methodological foundation.

The work remained exploratory and research-oriented. We did not train a supervised cuisine-classification model.

## 6. Hierarchical Clustering Using Frequent Ingredient Patterns

One of the major computational components of the analysis was the implementation of ingredient-pattern-based hierarchical clustering.

The methodology was inspired by the frequent-pattern-based clustering approach discussed in *Hierarchical Clustering of World Cuisines*.

Since the available CulinaryDB data did not contain the complete set of cooking-process and utensil information used in the original paper, our implementation used ingredient co-occurrence patterns.

### 6.1 Frequent Ingredient Co-occurrence Mining

We represented recipes as collections of ingredients.

Instead of considering each ingredient independently, we investigated combinations of ingredients that occurred together across recipes.

For each cuisine, we mined frequent ingredient co-occurrence patterns.

The minimum support threshold was set to 20%.

The support of an itemset was calculated as:

**Support(X) = Number of recipes containing X / Total number of recipes**

For example, if an ingredient combination appeared in at least 20% of the recipes belonging to a cuisine, it satisfied the selected minimum support threshold.

We retained patterns satisfying this threshold and used them to construct cuisine-level feature representations.

**Implementation note:** The report documents frequent-pattern mining and the 20% support threshold, but does not explicitly identify the exact frequent-pattern algorithm or its Python implementation. We therefore do not attribute the implementation to a specific algorithm such as FP-Growth without checking the source code.

### 6.2 Binary Feature-Vector Construction

After extracting frequent patterns, we represented cuisines using binary feature vectors.

Each feature corresponded to a frequent ingredient pattern.

The representation indicated whether a particular pattern was present or absent in the cuisine's extracted pattern set.

Conceptually:

- 1 represented the presence of a pattern.
- 0 represented its absence.

This converted categorical recipe-pattern information into a numerical representation that could be used for distance calculations and clustering.

### 6.3 Euclidean Distance-Based Hierarchical Clustering

We first calculated pairwise Euclidean distances between cuisine feature vectors.

The Euclidean distance between two vectors was calculated as:

**D(A,B) = sqrt(sum((A_i - B_i)^2))**

We then applied Hierarchical Agglomerative Clustering using average linkage.

The process worked as follows:

1. Initially, every cuisine was treated as an individual cluster.
2. Pairwise distances between cuisines were calculated.
3. The closest clusters were progressively merged.
4. Average linkage was used to determine the distance between clusters.
5. The merging process continued until the hierarchical structure was constructed.
6. The resulting hierarchy was visualised as a dendrogram.

### 6.4 Interpreting the Euclidean Dendrogram

We generated a hierarchical tree using Euclidean distance on frequent ingredient patterns.

The resulting dendrogram provided a visual representation of similarities between cuisines.

**Observations:**

- Cuisines merging at lower linkage heights shared more similar frequent ingredient patterns.
- European and American cuisines often clustered together, reflecting shared pantry ingredients.
- Several Asian cuisines, including Japanese, Korean, Chinese and Thai, appeared in related branches.
- Some relationships reflected ingredient overlap rather than geographical proximity.

This was an ingredient-only adaptation of the original hierarchical-clustering methodology, which also incorporated cooking processes and utensils.

## 7. Jaccard Distance-Based Hierarchical Clustering

We then used the same frequent-pattern representations to construct another hierarchical tree using Jaccard distance.

This allowed us to investigate whether changing the distance metric altered the relationships between cuisines.

### 7.1 Jaccard Distance Calculation

Jaccard distance measures dissimilarity based on the overlap between two sets.

**D_J(A,B) = 1 - |A intersection B| / |A union B|**

Unlike Euclidean distance, Jaccard distance focuses on shared versus total patterns.

It is particularly useful when working with binary presence/absence representations.

### 7.2 Clustering and Dendrogram Generation

We calculated Jaccard distances between the same binary frequent-pattern representations and generated another hierarchical dendrogram.

**Observations:**

- The resulting cluster structure differed from the Euclidean-based tree.
- Jaccard distance emphasised the overlap of frequent patterns.
- Geographically distant cuisines could appear together if they shared similar ingredient patterns.
- Some unexpected groupings could potentially reflect historical culinary influences.

### 7.3 Comparing the Two Distance Metrics

This experiment helped us understand that clustering results are not determined by the dataset alone.

They also depend on:

- How features are represented.
- Which patterns are selected.
- The distance metric.
- The linkage method.

The comparison demonstrated why evaluating more than one similarity metric is important when studying cuisine relationships.

## 8. Authenticity-Based Hierarchical Clustering

We also implemented the second major approach discussed in the hierarchical-clustering paper: authenticity-based cuisine clustering.

Unlike frequent pattern mining, which identifies recurring combinations, this method attempts to identify ingredients that are particularly characteristic of individual cuisines.

### 8.1 Ingredient Prevalence Calculation

We calculated ingredient prevalence for every cuisine.

The prevalence of an ingredient represented how frequently it appeared in the recipes belonging to that cuisine.

We then calculated the corresponding cross-cuisine mean prevalence.

### 8.2 Authenticity Score Calculation

We obtained authenticity profiles by subtracting the cross-cuisine mean prevalence from the prevalence of each ingredient in a particular cuisine.

Conceptually:

**Authenticity(c,i) = Prevalence(c,i) - Mean Prevalence(i)**

Where:

- c represents a cuisine.
- i represents an ingredient.
- Prevalence(c,i) represents the prevalence of ingredient i in cuisine c.
- Mean Prevalence(i) represents its average prevalence across cuisines.

This produced a cuisine-by-ingredient authenticity matrix.

Positive values indicated ingredients that were more prevalent in a cuisine than the cross-cuisine average.

Negative values indicated ingredients that were less prevalent than the cross-cuisine average.

### 8.3 Selecting Distinctive Ingredients

We selected the top 80 most distinctive ingredient features for the clustering analysis.

These features were used to represent each cuisine through its authenticity profile.

### 8.4 Euclidean Clustering on Authenticity Profiles

We calculated Euclidean distances between cuisine authenticity vectors and applied hierarchical clustering.

The resulting dendrogram represented cuisine similarity based on the distinctive ingredient profiles.

**Observations:**

- Authenticity-based clustering captured both over-represented and under-represented ingredients.
- Indian Subcontinent appeared closer to spice-heavy regions than to some geographically neighbouring cuisines.
- Canadian and French cuisines appeared relatively close, potentially reflecting historical culinary influences.
- The resulting hierarchy represented ingredient identity rather than geographical distance alone.

### 8.5 Understanding the Difference Between Frequent Patterns and Authenticity

This was an important conceptual distinction.

**Frequent pattern-based clustering asks:**

Which combinations of ingredients occur repeatedly within a cuisine?

**Authenticity-based clustering asks:**

Which ingredients are unusually prevalent or under-represented in a cuisine compared with their prevalence across cuisines?

The two methods therefore capture different aspects of culinary similarity.

We understood why analysing only common ingredients may fail to capture the distinctive characteristics of a cuisine.

## 10. K-Means Clustering and the Elbow Method

To compare hierarchical clustering with a partition-based clustering method, we also applied K-Means clustering to the frequent-pattern feature vectors.

### 10.1 K-Means Analysis

We applied K-Means for values of K ranging from 1 to 10.

For each value of K, we calculated the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS).

WCSS measures the sum of squared distances between observations and their assigned cluster centroids.

### 10.2 Elbow Plot

We plotted WCSS against the number of clusters.

As expected, WCSS decreased as K increased.

However, the resulting curve did not show a clear elbow.

### 10.3 Interpretation

The absence of a sharp elbow made it difficult to identify a single natural number of clusters.

This was consistent with the observation discussed in the original paper.

It also reinforced the usefulness of hierarchical clustering for investigating continuous relationships between cuisines without requiring us to specify the number of clusters beforehand.

## 14. Limitations and Analyses That Could Not Be Fully Reproduced

Although the analysis implemented several methods discussed in the research literature, some limitations remained because of the available dataset.

### 14.1 Missing Cooking-Process Information

The CulinaryDB CSV release used in this analysis did not provide the cooking-process information required for the full methodology in Sharma et al.

The original research used ingredients, processes and utensils to study cuisine relationships.

Our implementation used ingredient co-occurrence patterns only.

Therefore, the resulting dendrograms were simplified ingredient-only adaptations rather than exact reproductions of the original feature representation.

### 14.2 Missing Utensil Information

The dataset did not contain the utensil information required for the original multi-feature clustering methodology.

We therefore could not incorporate utensils into the frequent-pattern representations.

### 14.3 Missing Flavour-Molecule Profiles

CulinaryDB did not provide the chemical flavour-compound profiles needed to reproduce the food-pairing calculations from Singh and Bagler.

Consequently, we could not calculate:

- Shared flavour-molecule overlap between ingredients.
- Recipe-level chemical food-pairing scores.
- Cuisine-level food-pairing scores.
- Food-pairing Z-scores using randomised baselines.

These analyses would require external information from FlavorDB.

We deliberately did not substitute ingredient co-occurrence for chemical flavour compatibility.

### 14.4 Missing Geographical Coordinates

The analysis did not include geographical coordinates for the cuisines.

Therefore, we could not construct a separate geographical-distance dendrogram or quantitatively compare the clustering results with geographical distances.

Our interpretation of geographical relationships remained qualitative.

### 14.5 Ingredient-Only Frequent Pattern Representation

Our frequent-pattern clustering used ingredient combinations rather than the complete set of ingredients, processes and utensils used in the original research.

This meant that two cuisines could appear similar because of shared ingredients even if their preparation techniques were substantially different.

### 14.6 Ingredient State and Preparation Differences

The analysis was based on the ingredient entities available in CulinaryDB.

It did not fully represent how an ingredient's culinary role changes depending on whether it is raw, roasted, dried, ground, boiled or otherwise processed.

These distinctions could be important for a more detailed representation of cuisine identity.

### 14.7 Unequal Cuisine Representation

The dataset contained a significant imbalance in recipe counts.

USA alone contributed 16,118 recipes, while several other cuisines had much smaller collections.

This could affect ingredient prevalence estimates, frequent-pattern extraction and the stability of cuisine-specific profiles.

The Japanese subset, for example, contained 580 recipes, making its top ingredient rankings more sensitive to individual recipes.

### 14.8 Sensitivity to Minimum Support

Frequent-pattern extraction depended on a minimum support threshold of 20%.

Changing this threshold could change:

- The number of patterns extracted.
- The number of features in the binary vectors.
- The calculated distances between cuisines.
- The resulting dendrogram structure.

A complete sensitivity analysis across multiple support thresholds was not documented in this report.

### 14.9 Sensitivity to Feature Selection

Authenticity-based clustering used the top 80 distinctive ingredient features.

The resulting clusters could change if a different number of features were selected.

The report did not establish that 80 was a universally optimal feature count.

### 14.10 Distance-Metric Dependence

The Euclidean and Jaccard dendrograms produced different cluster structures.

This demonstrated that cuisine relationships depended partly on the distance metric.

We did not establish one metric as universally correct for measuring culinary similarity.

### 14.11 Limitations of Authenticity Profiles

Authenticity was calculated relative to the cross-cuisine mean prevalence.

This made the results dependent on the cuisines represented in the dataset and their relative recipe distributions.

The resulting score should therefore be interpreted as relative ingredient distinctiveness within this dataset, not as an absolute or universally accepted measure of culinary authenticity.

### 14.12 Limitations of Geographical and Historical Interpretation

Some clustering patterns appeared consistent with geographical or historical relationships.

However, the clustering analysis did not independently prove that geography, migration, trade or colonisation caused those similarities.

Such explanations would require additional historical and geographical evidence.

### 14.13 K-Means Limitations

The elbow plot did not reveal a clear optimal number of clusters.

Although WCSS decreased with increasing K, the curve did not show a sharp turning point.

Therefore, we could not confidently identify a single natural cluster count using the Elbow Method.

### 14.14 Correlation-Based Similarity Limitations

The authenticity heatmap used Pearson correlation between cuisine profiles.

Pearson correlation measures the strength of linear association between profile values. It does not establish that two cuisines have identical ingredient distributions or that they share a direct historical relationship.

### 14.15 No External Ground-Truth Validation

The resulting clusters were interpreted using known geographical and historical culinary relationships.

However, the report did not establish a quantitative external ground-truth benchmark for cuisine similarity.

Consequently, the dendrograms should be treated as exploratory representations rather than definitive classifications of world cuisines.

## 15. Important Dataset and Methodological Considerations

Several considerations affect how the results should be interpreted:

- Recipe counts were substantially imbalanced across cuisines, with the USA contributing 16,118 recipes.
- Ingredient-frequency measurements represented the number of recipes containing an ingredient, rather than total occurrences or quantities.
- The frequent-pattern clustering used a minimum support threshold of 20%, which influences the patterns retained.
- Authenticity-based clustering used the top 80 distinctive ingredient features, making the resulting structure dependent on feature selection.
- Euclidean and Jaccard distances produced different clustering structures, demonstrating the importance of the selected similarity metric.
- The analysis used the ingredient information available in CulinaryDB and did not include the complete cooking-process, utensil or flavour-molecule information used in related research.
- The source distribution of recipes could influence ingredient naming conventions and the representation of individual cuisines.

These considerations are important when interpreting the dendrograms and comparing the results with the original research papers.

This research exercise combined descriptive CulinaryDB analysis with computational methods for studying cuisine relationships.

We examined recipe characteristics, ingredient popularity, category composition, regional ingredient distributions and dataset provenance across 26 cuisines.

The experiments demonstrated that cuisine similarity can be represented in multiple ways and that the resulting relationships depend on feature selection, representation and distance metrics.
