## 1. Research Background and Objective

Following our detailed study of the paper *Universal Statistical Laws Governing Culinary Design*, we moved from understanding its theoretical framework to implementing its analytical methodology on the CocktailDB dataset.

In the previous research stage, we had studied the mathematical foundations of Zipf's law, Heaps' law, the Menzerath–Altmann law, nutrient distributions and the generative mechanisms proposed in the paper.

The next objective was to investigate whether these statistical regularities could also be observed in cocktail recipes.

The original paper investigated a large corpus of traditional recipes across multiple cuisines. CocktailDB, in contrast, represents a narrower domain consisting primarily of short drink recipes, with repeated base spirits, juices, liqueurs, sweeteners, bitters and garnishes.

We therefore treated the paper as a methodological reference rather than attempting to reproduce its numerical results.

The main objectives were:

1. Understand the structure and quality of CocktailDB.
2. Map the variables used in the reference paper to the available CocktailDB columns.
3. Develop an appropriate ingredient extraction and normalization pipeline.
4. Implement Zipf's law on ingredient frequencies.
5. Implement Heaps' law using incremental vocabulary growth.
6. Investigate the Menzerath–Altmann relationship using ingredient information content.
7. Examine whether nutrient distributions exhibit log-normal characteristics.
8. Explore ingredient co-occurrence and multivariate relationships.
9. Identify which analyses from the original paper could not be reproduced because of missing data.
10. Present the implementation, results and limitations to the professor.

The implementation was designed to allow CocktailDB to exhibit similar, different or statistically weak patterns without forcing it to match the reference paper.

## 2. Dataset Inspection and Initial Audit

The dataset contained 16,895 rows and nine columns:

- Name
- Name.1
- Category
- Ingredients
- Nutrients
- Ratings
- Votes
- Prep_Time
- Cuisine

The initial audit revealed several data-quality issues.

### 2.1 Duplicate and Redundant Fields

The Name and Name.1 columns were identical, with zero mismatches. Therefore, Name.1 was excluded from the analytical models.

We also identified:

- 17 additional exact duplicate rows.
- 23 additional Name + Ingredients duplicate records, including the exact duplicates.
- 1,144 recipe names appearing more than once.
- 1,131 names associated with different ingredient lists, which were retained as legitimate recipe variants.

The frequency corpus therefore contained 16,872 recipes.

We deliberately retained different ingredient formulations under the same recipe name because treating every repeated name as a duplicate would remove potentially meaningful recipe variations.

### 2.2 Missing-Value Analysis

We performed a column-wise missingness audit.

| Column | Missing values |
|---|---:|
| Cuisine | 97.7% |
| Nutrients | 66.3% |
| Category | 59.0% |
| Prep_Time | 58.6% |
| Ratings | 52.5% |
| Votes | 52.6% |
| Ingredients | 0% |

This immediately established that the dataset could not support every analysis from the reference paper.

Cuisine was particularly problematic. Only 393 recipes had non-missing cuisine information, and the labels also mixed drink types and geographical identities.

Consequently, cuisine-stratified statistical laws were not considered reliable.

### 2.3 Rating and Vote Cleaning

Votes were stored as text, sometimes containing comma separators.

We removed commas and converted the values into numeric form.

We also found 116 records where Rating = 0 and Votes = 0 simultaneously.

These records were treated as unrated rather than genuine zero-star ratings.

The original columns were preserved, while separate analysed fields were created.

No ratings, votes or textual fields were imputed.

### 2.4 Preparation-Time Parsing

Preparation times contained different formats, including:

- Seconds.
- Minutes.
- Hours.
- Days.
- Numeric ranges.
- Repeated phrases.
- Unitless numbers.

We implemented a parsing procedure to convert preparation times into minutes.

The conversion rules were:

- Seconds divided by 60.
- Hours multiplied by 60.
- Days multiplied by 1,440.
- Numeric ranges represented using their average.
- Whole repeated phrases removed.
- Unitless numbers interpreted as minutes.

All preparation strings were successfully parsed, but unusual values were retained and flagged rather than automatically deleted.

The maximum recorded preparation time was 20,170 minutes, illustrating the importance of investigating extreme observations.

## 3. Ingredient Extraction and Normalization

Ingredient extraction was one of the most important parts of the implementation because the statistical laws depend directly on ingredient identity and frequency.

The original dataset contained pipe-delimited ingredient strings, with quantities and measurement descriptions embedded in the text.

We split these strings into individual ingredient records and developed four normalization levels.

### 3.1 Surface Normalization

This involved lowercase conversion and whitespace normalization.

It retained most of the original ingredient descriptions.

### 3.2 Standard Normalization

This was the primary normalization used for the main statistical analyses.

It involved:

- Lowercase conversion.
- Whitespace normalization.
- Removal of leading and trailing quantities.
- Removal of measurement units.
- Mapping Cointreau to orange liqueur.
- Mapping Kahlua to coffee liqueur.

We deliberately did not collapse lime, lime juice and fresh lime juice into one entity.

### 3.3 Moderate Normalization

This additionally removed garnish and preparation descriptors.

### 3.4 Core Normalization

This was used for sensitivity analysis and included additional simplifications, such as merging ice variants and removing the descriptor fresh.

The four levels helped us understand how much ingredient identity decisions could influence the statistical results.

Under standard normalization, the corpus contained:

- 14,466 unique ingredients.
- 79,411 total ingredient mentions.
- 31,180 unique surface strings.
- 12,480 unique core-normalized strings.

Ingredient frequency was calculated as the number of distinct recipes containing an ingredient, without double-counting repeated mentions within the same recipe.

## 5. Exploratory Data Analysis

Before implementing the statistical laws, we examined the general structure of the cocktail corpus.

### 5.1 Recipe Size Distribution

We calculated the number of unique ingredients in each recipe.

The results were:

- Mean: 4.67 ingredients.
- Median: 4 ingredients.
- Standard deviation: 1.80.
- Interquartile range: 3.
- Minimum: 1.
- Maximum: 22.

We generated:

- Recipe-size histogram.
- Violin and box plots.
- Empirical cumulative distribution function.

The analysis showed that cocktail recipes were considerably shorter than the traditional food recipes studied in the reference paper.

This was important because recipe size is directly used in the Menzerath–Altmann analysis.

### 5.2 Category Distribution

Category information was available for 6,925 recipes, representing approximately 41% of the frequency corpus.

There were 429 distinct category labels.

We examined the top 20 categories and their distribution.

The labels were heterogeneous, mixing:

- Drink styles.
- Spirit families.
- Flavour families.
- Glassware-related descriptions.

Therefore, category analysis was restricted to records with available labels and was not treated as a globally representative classification.

### 5.3 Ingredient Popularity

The most frequently occurring normalized ingredient was vodka, appearing in 2,191 recipes.

A particularly important finding was that 10,626 ingredients occurred in only one recipe.

This represented 73.5% of the ingredient vocabulary.

The distribution demonstrated a small core of frequently reused ingredients and a long tail of rare ingredients.

### 5.4 Ratings and Votes

Among analysed ratings:

- Mean rating: 4.317.
- Median rating: 4.450.
- Standard deviation: 0.442.
- Number of analysed ratings: 7,890.

Votes were heavily right-skewed:

- Mean votes: 38.4.
- Median votes: 30.
- Maximum votes: 4,348.
- Skewness: 25.10.

We examined the relationship between rating and log-transformed votes.

The Spearman correlation was approximately -0.058.

This was statistically detectable but extremely weak in magnitude.

We also implemented a Bayesian-style vote-adjusted rating estimator:

Adjusted rating = (V × R + m × C) / (V + m)

Here, V represents votes, R represents the observed rating, C is the global mean rating and m is the smoothing parameter, set to 10.

The purpose was to prevent recipes with very few votes and unusually high ratings from automatically dominating the ranking.

### 5.5 Preparation-Time Analysis

The parsed preparation-time data had:

- Mean: 7.95 minutes.
- Median: 3 minutes.
- 95th percentile: 10 minutes.
- Maximum: 20,170 minutes.

We generated preparation-time histograms, log-scale plots and scatter plots against ingredient count and votes.

Spearman associations were:

- Preparation time versus ingredient count: ρ = 0.352.
- Preparation time versus votes: ρ = -0.290.

These were treated as observational associations rather than causal relationships.

### 5.6 Nutritional EDA

We parsed two different nutrient-string formats:

1. Compact format, such as `184 Calories|3g Fat|8g Carbs`.
2. Colon-based format, such as `Calories: 239 kcal|Carbohydrates: 14 g`.

Carbs and Carbohydrates were mapped to the same variable after checking their units.

We preserved different units where appropriate, including kcal, grams, milligrams and IU.

We did not assume serving-size standardization because it was not documented in the source data.

## 6. Implementation of the Statistical Laws

### 6.1 Zipf's Law

The first major implementation investigated the rank-frequency distribution of ingredients.

We calculated the number of distinct recipes containing each normalized ingredient.

Ingredients were sorted in descending order of frequency and assigned ranks.

The model was:

f(r) ∝ r^(-α)

We generated a log-log rank-frequency plot.

The primary descriptive fit used ranks 5–500.

Results:

- Zipf exponent α ≈ 0.998.
- R² ≈ 0.991.
- Number of fitted rank points: 496.

The unrestricted full-range fit gave an exponent of approximately 0.878 and R² of 0.884.

This showed that the curve was not a perfect straight line over the entire range.

We additionally used the `powerlaw` library to perform Clauset-style discrete power-law fitting on the frequency vector.

This produced:

- Frequency-distribution exponent: approximately 1.848.
- Minimum frequency: 2.
- KS distance: 0.356.
- Tail observations: 3,840.

We compared the power-law model against log-normal and exponential alternatives.

An important methodological distinction was that the rank-frequency exponent and the frequency-distribution MLE exponent are different quantities.

We therefore reported them separately.

**Conclusion:** CocktailDB exhibited a heavy-tailed, Zipf-like ingredient usage pattern, but a perfect power law was not established.

### 6.2 Heaps' Law

Heaps' law investigates the growth of unique vocabulary as additional recipes are included.

The model was:

V(R) = K × R^β

Where V(R) is the cumulative number of unique ingredients and R is the number of recipes.

We randomly shuffled the recipe corpus 100 times.

For each ordering:

1. Recipes were incrementally added.
2. A cumulative set of unique ingredients was maintained.
3. The number of unique ingredients was recorded.
4. Vocabulary growth was fitted in logarithmic space.

The estimated mean exponent was:

β = 0.738

The 95% permutation interval was:

[0.725, 0.751]

The curve showed sublinear vocabulary growth.

This indicated that new cocktail recipes increasingly reused ingredients already present in the corpus, although new ingredients continued to appear.

We compared the result with the reference paper's global exponent of approximately 0.56.

We did not treat the numerical difference as a failure. The two corpora differ in size, domain and ingredient vocabulary.

### 6.3 Menzerath–Altmann Law

We next investigated the relationship between recipe size and average ingredient information content.

Ingredient information was calculated as:

s(i) = -log p(i)

The average information content for a recipe of size L was calculated using the information content of its ingredients.

We fitted the model:

y(L) = a × L^b × exp(cL)

The analysis included:

- Recipe-size calculation.
- Ingredient probability estimation.
- Information-content calculation.
- Scatter plots.
- Binned average curves.
- Nonlinear least-squares fitting.
- Residual and sensitivity checks.

The Spearman correlation between recipe size and average ingredient information was approximately 0.101.

The analysis did not assume that the cocktail dataset would reproduce the reference paper's decreasing-then-increasing pattern.

A major issue was that cocktail recipes were much shorter, with a median ingredient count of four.

This meant that the available recipe-size range was substantially different from the global food corpus, making direct comparison of the curve's minimum difficult.

### 6.4 Nutrient Distribution Analysis

We investigated whether the nutrient quantities followed log-normal distributions.

Only positive nutrient values were used for logarithmic transformations and KS distribution fitting.

Zero values were counted separately.

We examined:

- Original-scale nutrient distributions.
- Log-transformed nutrient distributions.
- Log-space skewness.
- Candidate distribution fits.
- Kolmogorov–Smirnov distances.
- Nutrient correlations.

Candidate distributions included:

- Log-normal.
- Gamma.
- Weibull.
- Gaussian.
- Exponential.

The best KS candidates varied by nutrient:

| Nutrient | Best candidate |
|---|---|
| Calories | Log-normal |
| Carbohydrates | Gamma |
| Protein | Weibull |
| Fat | Log-normal |
| Sugar | Gamma |
| Sodium | Log-normal |

We did not claim that all cocktail nutrients followed a log-normal distribution.

The incomplete coverage and unknown serving-size standardization restricted the conclusions.

## 7. Ingredient Co-occurrence Analysis

In addition to reproducing the main statistical framework, we explored ingredient combinations.

We constructed a co-occurrence matrix using the top 40 ingredients.

The number of recipes containing each pair was counted.

We also calculated Pointwise Mutual Information:

PMI(i,j) = ln[p(i,j) / (p(i)p(j))]

A minimum joint occurrence threshold of 25 recipes was used to reduce rare-event inflation.

A network was generated using positive-PMI edges, with the displayed network using PMI greater than 0.4.

The initial network contained 166 edges.

Some prominent associations included:

- Irish cream – whipped cream.
- Coffee liqueur – Irish cream.
- Lemon – blue curaçao.
- Irish cream – milk.
- Triple sec – tequila.

We interpreted these as recipe co-occurrence patterns, not proof of flavour compatibility.

The analysis was useful for identifying recurring ingredient combinations and possible future research directions.

We also investigated selected relationships between:

- Ingredient count.
- Preparation time.
- Ratings.
- Votes.
- Calories.
- Sugar.
- Protein.

Spearman correlation was used as the primary association measure because many variables were skewed and contained outliers.

Bootstrap confidence intervals were generated using 400 resamples for key relationships.

Some of the strongest observed associations were:

| Variable pair | Spearman correlation |
|---|---:|
| Ingredient count vs calories | 0.25 |
| Ingredient count vs votes | -0.19 |
| Ingredient count vs protein | 0.14 |

We also performed PCA on eight standardized nutrient variables using 2,319 complete-case recipes.

The first principal component explained 61.5% of the variance, while the second explained 16.8%.

Together, the first two components explained 78.3%.

The PCA was treated as descriptive dimensionality reduction rather than a health score or clustering model.

## 9. Limitations and Problems Encountered

The first implementation revealed several important limitations.

### 9.1 Missing and Incomplete Metadata

Cuisine was 97.7% missing, preventing reliable cuisine-level analysis.

Category was approximately 59% missing, restricting category comparisons to the observed subset.

Nutrients were approximately 66.3% missing, making nutritional conclusions dependent on a much smaller subset.

### 9.2 Ingredient Normalization

The ingredient field contained quantities, units, preparation descriptions and alternative names.

Normalization was partly subjective.

Different normalization choices changed the size of the ingredient vocabulary and could influence Zipf, Heaps and Menzerath–Altmann estimates.

### 9.3 Smaller Corpus and Different Domain

CocktailDB contained 16,872 recipes in the frequency corpus, considerably fewer than the 118,083 recipes in the reference paper.

Cocktails also tend to contain fewer ingredients than traditional cooked dishes.

Therefore, the exponents and curve shapes were not expected to match exactly.

### 9.4 Zipf Fitting

The log-log curve was curved rather than perfectly linear.

The fitted exponent changed when the rank interval changed.

A high R² alone was not sufficient to establish a genuine power law.

### 9.5 Heaps Sampling

The vocabulary-growth curve depended on the order in which recipes were introduced.

Repeated random shuffling helped quantify this ordering variability, but it did not remove the limitations of the underlying dataset.

### 9.6 Menzerath–Altmann Recipe-Size Range

Cocktail recipes were short, with a median of four ingredients.

There were relatively few large recipes, limiting the ability to investigate whether the same non-monotonic relationship observed in the global food corpus existed in CocktailDB.

### 9.7 Nutrient Inconsistency

Nutrient strings appeared in multiple formats.

The source did not reliably specify serving sizes.

Extremely large nutrient values could represent batch quantities, source inconsistencies or other issues, but the CSV did not allow us to establish which explanation was correct.

### 9.8 Co-occurrence Is Not Flavour Chemistry

PMI and ingredient co-occurrence reveal whether ingredients are used together more frequently than expected from their individual prevalence.

They do not establish sensory compatibility or shared flavour compounds.

This would require external flavour-compound information.

### 9.9 Observational Bias

The dataset likely combines heterogeneous sources, including recipe blogs, rating websites and nutrition-labelled pages.

Ratings and votes measure user attention and reported opinions, not objective culinary quality.

### 9.10 Missing Variables from the Reference Paper

The dataset did not provide:

- Structured cooking techniques.
- Utensils.
- Instruction-step complexity.
- Reliable cuisine labels.
- Full NER-extracted ingredient attributes.
- Standardized per-serving nutrient values.

Therefore, these analyses could not be directly reproduced.

After implementing the paper's methodology, we prepared a detailed 39-page research report documenting the complete analytical workflow, results, graphs, statistical estimates and limitations.

The report was presented to Professor Ganesh Bagler.

The presentation covered:

1. How the original paper's statistical questions were mapped onto CocktailDB.
2. The dataset audit and preprocessing decisions.
3. Ingredient extraction and normalization.
4. Recipe-size and ingredient-frequency distributions.
5. Zipf fitting and the interpretation of the exponent.
6. Heaps vocabulary-growth analysis.
7. Menzerath–Altmann information-content analysis.
8. Nutrient distribution fitting.
9. Co-occurrence networks and PMI.
10. Multivariate correlations and PCA.
11. The differences between the cocktail dataset and the global recipe corpus.
12. Limitations and analyses that could not be reproduced.

The discussion was important because it allowed us to evaluate whether the graphs communicated the statistical findings correctly and whether our interpretations were supported by the actual data.

We identified several areas requiring further scrutiny:

- Whether the Zipf exponent was stable across different rank intervals.
- Whether a high log-log R² concealed curvature.
- Whether the Heaps analysis could better communicate variability across random permutations.
- How to interpret the colours in correlation heatmaps and distinguish correlation matrices from count matrices.
- How to make highly skewed distributions and extreme values easier to interpret.
- Whether the ingredient network should be examined hierarchically.
- Whether network edge widths and node colours had precise statistical meanings.
- Whether a full network backbone could provide more information than the selected top-40 PMI network.

These observations became the basis for the subsequent refinement stage.

## 11. Planned Improvements for the Next Meeting

We planned to revisit the original report rather than immediately expand into unrelated analyses.

The next phase would focus on improving the statistical evidence, visualization and interpretation.

### 11.1 Zipf Robustness

- Fit the rank-frequency relationship over ranks 5–500, 5–1000 and 5–2000.
- Examine whether the estimated exponent changes with the fitting interval.
- Investigate residual curvature.
- Compare normalized ingredients against literal quantity-bearing strings.
- Improve the explanation of the difference between rank-frequency OLS and frequency-distribution MLE.

### 11.2 Heaps Visualization

- Generate boxplots of vocabulary size at fixed recipe counts.
- Show the distribution of vocabulary growth across random orderings.
- Display the fitted beta values across permutations.
- Explain why the final vocabulary count must be identical at the full corpus size.

### 11.3 Correlation and Heatmap Interpretation

- Add numerical values to correlation heatmaps.
- Use a diverging colour scale centred at zero.
- Display the number of observations for each pair.
- Investigate why certain nutrient correlations are strong while popularity-related correlations remain weak.
- Avoid interpreting a small p-value as a large effect.

### 11.4 Distribution Visualization

- Generate paired raw and log-transformed plots.
- Investigate unusually large nutrient and preparation-time values.
- Retain valid zero values using log1p where appropriate.
- Use density-oriented scatter visualizations for heavily overlapping observations.

### 11.5 Hierarchical Co-occurrence

- Apply hierarchical clustering to ingredient recipe-membership vectors.
- Investigate Jaccard similarity and distance.
- Generate a heatmap with dendrograms.
- Evaluate whether the clusters correspond to meaningful ingredient co-use groups.

### 11.6 Improved Network Representation

- Give edge widths a mathematically defined mapping.
- Separate ingredient frequency from association strength.
- Use consistent node colours.
- Investigate the complete co-occurrence network rather than only the top 40 ingredients.

### 11.7 Network Backbone and Statistical Robustness

- Construct the full ingredient network before applying selection thresholds.
- Investigate the disparity filter.
- Examine multiple alpha thresholds.
- Compare retained and non-retained edges.
- Test alternative significance rules.
- Investigate multiple-testing correction.
- Compare the disparity-filter result against an ingredient-occurrence independence null.

The goal was to understand whether the apparent network structure was robust or was largely a consequence of arbitrary visualization and filtering choices.

## 12. Overall Outcome

This first implementation successfully translated the major statistical questions from *Universal Statistical Laws Governing Culinary Design* into a cocktail-specific analytical workflow.

We established that CocktailDB exhibits a concentrated ingredient-frequency distribution and sublinear vocabulary growth.

However, the Menzerath–Altmann relationship and nutrient distributions did not reproduce every pattern observed in the original food corpus.

The implementation also exposed limitations in ingredient normalization, missing metadata, source heterogeneity, extreme values and the interpretation of ingredient co-occurrence networks.

Most importantly, the first report provided a reproducible baseline against which subsequent modifications could be evaluated.

The next stage was therefore planned as a systematic robustness and interpretation exercise: testing the stability of the original results, improving visualizations, introducing hierarchical co-occurrence analysis and examining the full ingredient network.
